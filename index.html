Abstract

Long-horizon mathematical reasoning fails less often because a model cannot produce a valid next step than because an agent fails to maintain and expose the right semantic state across many iterations. Left unmanaged, this produces research-level proofs that are locally convincing yet globally incomplete: a key lemma unproved, an assumption unchecked, a citation unsupported, or a computational claim unverified. We present Research Math Agents (RMA), an agentic framework for long-horizon proof development built around a persistent, typed research store and an orchestrator that compiles operation-specific context from that store. The Research Context Orchestrator is the central state-management layer between the persistent research store and each locally scoped proof operation: it retrieves task-relevant artifacts, compiles them into a bounded context, invokes the appropriate operation, and writes the resulting proof edits, issue updates, literature notes, plans, or evaluations back to the store. This process is designed to keep proof revisions, unresolved issues, prior attempts, literature, and evaluations available across rounds while exposing only task-relevant state to each local operation. We evaluate RMA across complementary research-level settings using independent expert evaluation, blind mathematician review, LLM-based benchmark evaluation, and Lean 4 kernel verification. RMA achieves a 42.5% solve rate on the independently evaluated SOOHAK Challenge Hard set, obtains 8 of 10 correct solutions on First Proof B1 and 8 of 10 passing solutions on B2 under human-expert evaluation, and verifies 213 of 300 sampled Research Solved targets in Formal Conjectures with the Lean 4 kernel. These results support structured state and context orchestration as useful components for long-horizon proof development, while comparisons against independently developed systems remain subject to differences in inference budgets, backbones, and evaluation protocols.

Method

Research-level proofs usually fail by omission: the text reads as finished, but a required part of the argument is still missing. Repairing such a gap needs more than another critique of the current draft. It needs the unresolved claim itself, what depends on it, what has already been tried, and which literature applies. RMA therefore treats proof development as a state-management problem and organizes it into three layers.

Persistent Research Store. Proof development is organized into research rounds; a round examines the current proof, identifies and prioritizes unresolved obligations, performs a bounded collection of research and repair operations, revises the proof, and evaluates the result. Each problem has one store S=(Π,I,M,L,K,H,E)\mathcal{S}=(\Pi,\mathcal{I},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H},\mathcal{E})S=(Π,I,M,L,K,H,E): proof artifacts and their revision history, open and resolved issues, structured meeting records, literature records (when literature grounding is enabled), mathematical concepts and background knowledge, strategic insights, and evaluation records. The store is initialized from the problem and persists across rounds; execution may stop early when the proof is judged complete or the system ceases to make measurable progress across two consecutive rounds.

Research Context Orchestrator. An individual LM call should not receive the entire store: context windows are bounded, and models do not reliably use all information in long inputs. The orchestrator takes a problem ppp, a round budget NRN_RNR​, a per-round issue budget bbb, and a per-call context budget BBB, and for every call compiles the operation’s instructions, its task, the current proof, the records linked to it, and the operation’s own recent outputs. \textscPrefixToBudget\textsc{PrefixToBudget}\textscPrefixToBudget then removes whole, addressable records in ascending priority order until the observation fits within BBB, so an operation never receives a lemma statement cut mid-sentence. Issues are discovered by four complementary analyses: an LM gap critic, a deterministic structural and dependency checker, a semantic completeness judge, and a fidelity analysis that asks whether the proof’s reading of the problem is the intended one rather than one that trivializes it. Each issue receives a severity (P0–P3) by its effect on proof validity, and open issues are ordered by severity, then by dependency impact (the number of unresolved downstream claims resting on the affected claim), both computed deterministically from a claim-dependency graph rebuilt every round. The solver repairs the first bbb issues one at a time with anchored patches; the next critic round re-scans the full proof, so any remaining or reintroduced defect is recorded again. Literature grounding is an optional module; meetings and coordinated revision handle strategy-level changes that local repairs cannot reach; a concept glossary standardizes terminology; and the loop terminates through \textscSolved\textsc{Solved}\textscSolved only when the semantic completeness gate passes and no open issue is correctness-critical.

Locally scoped operations. The critic, solver, optional literature researcher, meeting, reviser, and evaluator each receive only their compiled observation and return a typed artifact that passes a deterministic write-back gate into the corresponding component of S\mathcal{S}S. This common interface keeps the operations modular while their outputs accumulate into a single persistent, auditable record of proof development.

Results

We evaluate on four benchmarks with complementary evaluators: independent third-party expert grading (SOOHAK Challenge Hard), blind review by mathematicians (First Proof B1 and B2), the benchmark’s own LLM-judge protocol (ResearchMath), and Lean 4 kernel verification (Formal Conjectures). The default backbone is Claude Opus 4.8, except for First Proof B1, which was run earlier with Claude Opus 4.6. Sampling for SOOHAK, First Proof, and ResearchMath is fixed by their organizers, not by us.

SOOHAK Challenge Hard has 40 non-public, contamination-resistant graduate and research-adjacent problems. Cost, scaling, and ablations

The studies below use 30 held-out ResearchMath problems that the benchmark annotates as solved, drawn from a pool of 1,169 such questions with a fixed seed and checked to be disjoint from every ResearchMath problem the system was ever run on. Every configuration, including Generic Iterative Revision, runs under the same hard budget of $100 per problem and is scored by a continuous 0–10 LLM judge (the mean of Claude Opus 4.8, GPT-5.2, and Gemini 3.1 Pro).

Cost and scalability. Over the tested B2 budget points, the pass count reaches its maximum of 8 of 10 at the $1,850 headline run and stays there at $3,571. Reading off what a run capped at NR=rN_R=rNR​=r rounds would deliver, the judge score rises from 4.5 at one round to 8.5 at six (4.5, 5.7, 6.7, 7.5, 8.1, 8.5), with per-round gains falling from 1.2 to 0.4.

Matched-harness memory ablation. To isolate the contribution of structured persistent memory, the RMA harness is held fixed (backbone, prompts, operations, tools, issue ranking, stopping rule, selection policy, context budget, and the $100 per-problem budget) while only the representation and retrieval of cross-round memory change: full RMA keeps typed, individually addressable records with record-level retrieval, and the summary-memory variant keeps the research state in summarized form. The LLM score is the mean and standard deviation over three repeated runs; the human column is a separate blinded mathematician Pass/Fail evaluation of one paired set of 30 outputs per configuration.

Broader architectural control. Generic Iterative Revision (GIR) removes the store and the orchestrator entirely and keeps the backbone, tools, and budget: each iteration is one LM call that sees only the problem, its own previous output, and a fixed window of recent iterations, and the loop runs until a call returns NO ISSUES or the budget is exhausted. On the held-out set it scores 4.5 under its default output selection and 5.50 ± 0.10 when given RMA’s best-proof selection rule. On First Proof B2, under the same $1,850 total budget as the headline run ($185 per problem) and the same blind Pass/Fail protocol, GIR passes 4 of 10 problems against RMA’s 8 of 10.

Component ablations. The largest degradations from the full score of 8.5 occur when the system loses the mechanisms that preserve and expose research state: a stateless store gives 2.6, and dumping the full state into context instead of orchestrating it gives 3.4. Disabling structured literature grounding gives 4.2, FIFO issue repair 5.4, and removing meetings and coordinated revision 6.4. Disabling one critic analysis at a time gives 5.4 (structural checker), 5.8 (semantic completeness judge), 6.1 (LM gap critic), and 6.4 (fidelity analysis); dropping the concept glossary, strategic insights, or evaluator feedback gives 6.8, 7.2, and 7.5. Generic Iterative Revision at the same budget scores 4.5, so exactly the three configurations that break persistent state, bounded context selection, or literature grounding fall below a plain critique-and-revise loop.

On the memory and context ladders the step from Improved to Full (3.7 and 2.8) is larger than the step from Basic to Improved (2.2 and 2.3): keeping only the last round, or truncating the full state to fit the budget, recovers less than half of the gap. Repeated runs of the decisive configurations are consistent with the plotted ordering: full RMA 8.53 ± 0.15, severity-only ordering 7.23 ± 0.15, context dumping 3.47 ± 0.21, and the stateless variant 2.63 ± 0.25. Two interventions need qualification: context dumping changes request size and completion behavior as well as information selection, and disabling the literature operation removes structured retrieval and persistence rather than all web access, since the remaining operations keep their web tools.

Interpretation and scope. These ablations are mechanism-sensitivity analyses under a fixed $100-per-problem regime, not estimates of deployment-scale effect sizes or statistical rankings among nearby variants; conclusions are not drawn from small differences between single-run configurations. The central claim about structured persistent state does not rely on the LLM judge alone, since the matched-harness comparison is independently evaluated by blinded mathematicians (25 of 30 against 16 of 30 passes).
